The Problem — What We Set Out to Solve

GoEMed operates a direct-pay healthcare marketplace offering transparent, upfront procedure pricing. The business opportunity at the center of this project is health risk intelligence: if GoEMed can identify a user's elevated risk for cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or cancer, it can proactively surface relevant procedures and preventive care — all with transparent pricing. Health awareness drives procedure discovery, and procedure discovery drives GoEMed revenue.

The obstacle is data. Building a health risk prediction model requires patient health records — lab values, diagnoses, medications, lifestyle factors. Real patient data is protected under HIPAA. For a direct-pay marketplace without existing clinical relationships, obtaining real records requires months of data use agreement negotiations, IRB approval, and de-identification processes that still carry residual re-identification risk. For a startup moving quickly, this path is not viable.

?
The Question
How do we build a clinically rigorous health risk prediction model without access to real patient data?

This project's answer: generate synthetic patient data — statistically realistic artificial records calibrated to real population health statistics — and train a clinical language model on it. No real patient records ever enter the training pipeline. No HIPAA exposure. No data use agreements. The model learns from synthetic data that behaves like real data without being real data.

✓
Why a Small Language Model?
Real GoEMed users have incomplete information — they may know their age and family history but not their HbA1c. Language models handle missing inputs gracefully by leveraging context from clinical pre-training; tabular models degrade significantly without a complete feature vector.

The Generator — How We Made Fake Patients

The synthetic patient generator produces statistically realistic patient records through five sequential steps, each grounded in published statistical methodology. Running across 8 CPU cores, it generates 100,000 records in 11 seconds.

Step 1 — Demographic Sampling

Age, sex, and race are sampled from Census ACS 2022 population weights: Non-Hispanic White 59.4%, Hispanic 18.5%, Non-Hispanic Black 12.4%, NH Asian 6.0%, Other 3.7%.

Step 2 — Gaussian Copula Feature Sampling

Clinical features must be sampled jointly — a patient with BMI 45 but blood pressure 95/60 is physically impossible. The Gaussian copula preserves the pairwise correlation structure from NHANES by fitting 12 stratum-specific correlation matrices (6 races × 2 sexes), verifying Positive Semi-Definiteness via eigenvalue analysis, and using Cholesky decomposition to sample correlated standard normal variables. These are converted to uniform marginals via the normal CDF and inverted through each feature's fitted distribution (log-normal for BMI, normal for BP, gamma for fasting glucose).

Step 3 — Binary Features from BRFSS Rates

Smoking, physical activity, alcohol use, family history, snoring, and BP treatment are sampled as binary variables with probabilities drawn from BRFSS 2024 rates, adjusted for age, sex, and race where documented disparities exist.

Step 4 — Label Assignment via Clinical Risk Models

Nine validated clinical risk equations are evaluated on each record and used as Bernoulli parameters for label assignment:

  • Framingham Risk Score — 10-year CVD risk (Circulation 2008)
  • ACC/AHA ASCVD Pooled Cohort — 10-year ASCVD risk (JACC 2013)
  • FINDRISC Score — 10-year T2DM risk (Diabetologia 2003)
  • CKD-EPI (2021 race-free) — eGFR and CKD staging (NEJM 2021)
  • STOP-BANG Score — OSA risk (Anesthesiology 2008)
  • JNC8 / ACC-AHA 2017 — Hypertension diagnosis
  • ADA Diagnostic Criteria — Diabetes and Prediabetes
  • CDC BMI Classification — Obesity classes
  • Charlson Comorbidity Index — Comorbidity burden

Step 5 — Comorbidity Cascades

Biologically plausible comorbidity cascades reflect documented disease co-occurrence: T2DM raises CKD risk 25%, Hypertension raises CVD risk 10%, severe Obesity raises OSA risk 15%.

T²
Hotelling's T² — Applied Multivariate Analysis
Applied from Applied Multivariate Analysis coursework, Hotelling's T² is the multivariate generalization of the two-sample t-test — testing whether the entire vector of feature means is equal between synthetic and real data simultaneously, accounting for correlations between features. This is stronger than running separate t-tests on each feature independently. The normalized T² of 0.034 (well below 1.0) confirms no systematic bias across any combination of features.

The Model — Training Bio_ClinicalBERT

Prompt Serialization

Each synthetic patient record was converted from a row of numbers into a natural language sentence using five randomly selected template styles — clinical note, narrative paragraph, abbreviated EHR, structured list, and risk-focused. For example:

Patient: 58-year-old Black male. BMI: 31.4 kg/m². Blood pressure: 148/92 mmHg.
HbA1c: 7.2%. Fasting glucose: 142 mg/dL. On antihypertensive medication.
Family history: diabetes, CVD.

Fine-Tuning Configuration

Bio_ClinicalBERT (110M parameters, pre-trained on 880 million words of MIMIC III clinical notes) was loaded with a new 11-label multi-label classification head. Training used Binary Cross-Entropy loss with class-frequency inverse weighting, AdamW optimizer, cosine learning rate schedule, 5 epochs on 20,000 records with batch size 8 on Apple M2 MPS.

Power Analysis — SLM Saturates at 1,000 Records

Training at 5 dataset sizes revealed that Bio_ClinicalBERT reaches peak performance (AUROC 0.9861) with just 1,000 records and shows virtually no improvement with 20× more data. XGBoost follows a classical learning curve, improving from 0.76 to 0.80 as data increases — confirming that dataset size is a binding constraint for tabular models but not for pre-trained language models. This is the decisive advantage of transfer learning from clinical pre-training.

!
Identified Limitation — Hypertension Over-prediction
During API testing, the model assigned 99% hypertension probability to a 28-year-old female with normal BP (110/70 mmHg). Text-based language models don't process numeric thresholds with tabular precision. Recommended fix: hybrid strategy using XGBoost for hard-threshold conditions (Hypertension, Diabetes, Metabolic Syndrome) and Bio_ClinicalBERT for complex conditions (CVD Risk, Depression, Sleep Apnea, COPD).

Fairness Audit — 78.8% Pass Rate

Model performance was evaluated across 5 race groups, 2 sexes, and 4 age groups — 33 demographic subgroup checks. Any AUROC gap over 5 percentage points was flagged. Seven gaps were flagged; all seven have clinically justified explanations:

  • CVD Risk — Sex gap (19.8pp): ASCVD Pooled Cohort Equations use sex-stratified coefficients by design. Male and female CVD risk profiles differ in the literature.
  • Colorectal Cancer — Race gap (12.8pp): SEER race-stratified incidence rates embedded in the generator. Reflects real epidemiology.
  • CVD Risk — Age gap (13.1pp): Very low CVD prevalence under 45 reduces classifier signal.
  • COPD — Age gap (9.7pp): COPD is rare under age 45; low prevalence limits AUROC.
  • CKD — Age gap (6.8pp): CKD prevalence rises sharply with age.
  • COPD — Race gap (6.9pp): Smoking patterns vary significantly by race in BRFSS data.
  • OSA — Sex gap (6.5pp): Male predominance reflects STOP-BANG score sex weighting.

None of the seven flagged gaps represent model bias introduced by the synthetic data generator. Every gap has a clinically justified explanation rooted in real epidemiological patterns. The model is learning real health disparities, not amplifying stereotypes.

Deployment — The Live API on AWS

The trained Bio_ClinicalBERT model is deployed as a production-grade REST API on Amazon Web Services, accessible at http://52.91.134.254:8000. A GoEMed application sends a patient profile as JSON to /predict; the FastAPI server converts it to natural language, runs Bio_ClinicalBERT inference, and returns probability scores for all 11 conditions in under 700ms.

What's Next — Roadmap for GoEMed

The project exceeded the original proposal scope. Three priority enhancements are planned for v2:

1
Geographic Health Risk Intelligence — Cancer Alley
State-specific colorectal cancer multipliers computed from SEER 2018-2022 for all 50 states are stored in configs/regional_cancer_multipliers.yaml on the feature/regional-cancer-risk branch. Cancer Alley states (Louisiana 1.21×, Mississippi 1.25×) will be surfaced to GoEMed users as an explicit regional risk layer on top of the demographic health disparity signal already embedded in NHANES/BRFSS data. No other direct-pay marketplace currently offers location-aware health risk scoring.
2
Iron Deficiency Anemia & Heart Failure Labels
Adding hemoglobin from NHANES CBC files enables an anemia label (WHO criteria: Hgb < 12 g/dL women, < 13 g/dL men) and a heart failure risk label driven by anemia, CKD, hypertension, and existing CVD. This surfaces iron panel testing as a cardiac risk mitigation tool — a clinically underappreciated relationship in consumer health platforms.
3
Production HTTPS Deployment
Add Application Load Balancer with ACM TLS certificate for HTTPS, ECS Auto Scaling (70% CPU scale-up, 30% scale-down), API key authentication, and stable DNS name. These are infrastructure enhancements only — model and inference logic unchanged.
4
Hybrid Inference Strategy
Use XGBoost for hard-threshold conditions (Hypertension, Diabetes, Metabolic Syndrome) where numeric precision matters, and Bio_ClinicalBERT for complex conditions (CVD Risk, Depression, Sleep Apnea, COPD) where contextual reasoning adds value. Eliminates the identified hypertension over-prediction limitation.
